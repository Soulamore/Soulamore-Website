Soulamore Hosting Bandwidth Analysis
Executive Summary:
 
Soulamore’s unexpected Firebase Hosting egress can have multiple causes beyond SPA rewrites and brute-force scraping. Major culprits include image hotlinking, oversized assets, poor caching, endless client polling or loops, redirect loops, exposed files, and bots probing hidden domains. Each issue creates unnecessary data transfer – for example, hotlinked images or large uncompressed videos directly inflate bandwidth. We systematically analyze each root cause category, showing
 
how it inflates egress
,
 
how to detect it via logs and analytics
, and
 
precise mitigations with config examples
. We also provide a prioritized checklist (executive actions, immediate fixes first), tables comparing impact/difficulty, and a Mermaid flowchart of the mitigation workflow. Key actions include enabling Cloudflare protections (hotlinking, bot filters, WAF), optimizing assets and cache-control headers, fixing config (redirects, rewrites), and locking down unused domains. Implementing these can cut tens to hundreds of gigabytes of unwanted traffic.
1) Hotlinking and Third-Party Embedding
When other sites embed your images/videos (direct URLs on soulamore.com), every view on
 
their
 
site triggers data from your origin. This “hotlinking” directly boosts your egress. Cloudflare notes that enabling Hotlink Protection “prevents your images from being used by other sites, which can reduce the bandwidth consumed by your origin server”
[1]
.
Detection:
 
Check the
 
referer
 
field in logs. In Firebase’s Hosting logs (via Cloud Logging), each entry has an
 
httpRequest.referer
 
(if the referer is not blank)
[2]
. Filter for requests where
 
referer
 
is external (not your domain). In Cloudflare Analytics or Firewall Events (Logpush), look for HTTP requests for images where
 
Client.Request.Referer
 
is empty or from other domains. For example, a Cloud Logging query:
resource.type="firebase_domain" jsonPayload.requestUrl=~"\\.(png|jpe?g|gif)$" AND jsonPayload.referer!~"soulamore.com"
This finds image hits with an external referer. An alert could be set if >X% of image requests have foreign referers. Also, sudden spikes of image requests from unusual countries may hint at hotlinking.
Mitigation:
 
Enable Cloudflare’s Hotlink Protection feature
[1]
 
or create a Firewall rule blocking requests where the
 
Referer
 
header is not your domain. For example, a Cloudflare WAF rule:
(http.request.uri.path matches "^/.*\.(png|jpe?g|gif|svg)$") 
  and (not http.request.referer contains "soulamore.com")
→ block
This blocks image requests unless the referer is your site (Google crawler’s blank referer is usually allowed by Cloudflare by default). You can also whitelist specific domains (e.g. Google image cache) via “hotlink-ok” directories
[3]
. In
 
firebase.json
, there’s no direct hotlink setting, but you can add an
 
X-Robots-Tag: noindex, nofollow
 
header (shown below) to discourage indexing of raw image URLs by bots.
Estimated Savings:
 
Hotlinked images can account for
 
10–30%+
 
of origin bandwidth on image-heavy sites. Blocking external embedding can directly eliminate that overhead.
2) Large/Unoptimized Assets (Images, Video, Fonts, Bundles)
Issue:
 
Oversized files (uncompressed images/videos/fonts/JS) multiply bandwidth. Every user or bot download of a large file counts against egress. Large image files, high-res videos, or giant JS/CSS bundles dramatically increase data transfer.
How it inflates egress:
 
For example, a 5 MB image loaded 1,000 times = 5 GB egress. Unoptimized media (PNG vs compressed JPEG/WebP) or un-minified bundles can be several times larger than necessary.
Detection:
 
Use Firebase Hosting logs and Cloudflare analytics to find top contributors by size. In Cloud Logging:
resource.type="firebase_domain"
jsonPayload.responseSize>1000000
| top 10 by responseSize
This shows largest files served. In Cloudflare Analytics, check
 
Traffic > Top Hosts
 
for your domain and file types. High
 
responseSize
 
in logs indicates heavy files
[4]
. Also use Lighthouse or web.dev tools to audit asset sizes.
Mitigation:
 
Compress and resize images (e.g. convert JPEGs to WebP or use Cloudflare Polish
[5]
). For example, Cloudflare’s Polish (Pro/Business) automatically strips metadata and applies lossy/lossless compression, serving smaller images without code changes
[5]
. Videos should be external (YouTube/R3) or serve adaptive bitrate. Subset and compress web fonts. Minify and code-split JS/CSS (avoid shipping an entire 1 MB+ React bundle if not needed). For bundling, ensure tree-shaking and gzip (Firebase CDN auto-compresses gzip by
 
Accept-Encoding
).
Cloudflare Config:
 
Enable
 
Polish
 
(lossless or lossy) on images. Enable
 
Brotli
 
compression (Cloudflare setting) to compress text assets.
firebase.json:
 
Serve pre-optimized images; ensure you don’t upload huge originals.
Nginx/Other:
 
(If using custom proxy) use
 
gzip_static
 
or image optimization modules.
Estimated Savings:
 
Typical image optimization can cut image sizes by ~30–70%. Overall, optimizing media and bundles can
 
halve
 
bandwidth from assets.
3) Missing or Incorrect Cache-Control Headers
Issue:
 
If static files aren’t cached (or are set to
 
no-cache
/
no-store
), the CDN and browsers re-fetch them each time. Firebase’s CDN requires a proper
 
Cache-Control
 
header to store content at the edge
[6]
. Without it, every request is “uncached” (billed at uncached rate).
How it inflates egress:
 
Uncached responses always hit the origin. For example, if an image is 1 MB and users reload the page 100 times, that’s 100 MB egress instead of 1 MB (if cached after first).
Detection:
 
Check logs for
 
cacheHit:false
. In Cloud Logging, filter
 
httpRequest.cacheHit=false AND httpRequest.status=200
. Also filter for static file paths repeatedly requested (large responseSize with
 
cacheHit=false
). In Cloudflare analytics, see
 
Cache
 
reports (cache hit ratios).
Threshold/Alert:
 
Set an alert if cache-hit ratio drops below a threshold (e.g. <90%).
Mitigation:
 
Set aggressive Cache-Control. In
 
firebase.json
, add headers rules:
"headers": [
  {
    "source": "**/*.@(jpg|jpeg|png|gif|svg|webp)",
    "headers": [{"key": "Cache-Control","value": "public,max-age=31536000,immutable"}]
  },
  {
    "source": "**/*.@(js|css)",
    "headers": [{"key": "Cache-Control","value": "public,max-age=31536000,immutable"}]
  },
  {
    "source": "**/*.@(woff|woff2|ttf|eot)",
    "headers": [{"key": "Cache-Control","value": "public,max-age=31536000,immutable"}]
  },
  {
    "source": "**/*.html",
    "headers": [{"key": "Cache-Control","value": "public,max-age=300,s-maxage=600"}]
  }
]
This caches images/fonts/js for 1 year (immutable), HTML for shorter periods. Ensure you use fingerprinting (file names with hashes) so long cache is safe. Cloudflare will honor these headers by default. Remember, as [38†L1537-L1544] notes, CDN only caches responses with a
 
Cache-Control: max-age/s-maxage
 
header
 
and
 
size ≤10 MiB
[6]
, so also break up or compress files >10 MB.
Estimated Savings:
 
Proper caching can reduce origin egress for repeat visits by
 
80–90%
 
on static assets. By caching JS/CSS/images, you avoid re-downloading megabytes per view.
4) Infinite Polling/Websocket Loops or Client-Side Fetch Loops
Issue:
 
A bug in client code (e.g. setInterval polling every second, recursive fetch without pause, WebSocket emitting data rapidly) can cause constant traffic even without user interaction.
How it inflates egress:
 
Each poll or websocket message may return some payload. A poll every second = 60 requests/minute. Even a 10 KB JSON response yields 600 KB/minute per user. Multiply by bots or idle tabs, it adds up.
Detection:
 
Use logs to find rapid repeated requests from the same IP/UA in short time intervals. In Cloud Logging, group by
 
remoteIp
 
and count requests per minute:
resource.type="firebase_domain"
| stats count() by jsonPayload.remoteIp, bin(1m)
A threshold (e.g. >100 requests/min from one IP) may indicate a runaway loop. Alternatively, filter for endpoints known to poll. High
 
response_count
 
per unique IP in Monitoring can alert.
Mitigation:
 
Fix the code. Reduce polling frequency or switch to WebSockets/Server-Sent Events. Add flags to stop polls when not needed (e.g. on page blur). Ensure error cases stop retry loops. On the CDN side, no simple fix other than rate-limiting at Cloudflare. You can set a Cloudflare Rate Limiting rule on the API endpoint to allow X req/min per IP and block excess. Example (Cloudflare dashboard):
Create a Rate Limiting rule for
 
*.soulamore.com/api/
 
with threshold 60/min, block for 1h.
Estimated Savings:
 
Capping polling to once per minute (vs once per second) cuts related bandwidth by
 
~60×
 
for that feature. Even simple rate-limiting can eliminate large percentages of this egress.
5) Misconfigured Redirects (Infinite Loops)
Issue:
 
Redirect loops multiply requests. If page A redirects to B and back to A, each initial visit triggers multiple HTTP requests (and responses). Even a double-redirect wastes bandwidth.
How it inflates egress:
 
Example: A → B → A with 301/302. A single user hit on A becomes 2 extra round-trips. If the loop repeats indefinitely, it could drain hundreds of KB per visit.
Detection:
 
Look for high
 
status
 
in 3xx in logs and repeated sequences. In Cloud Logging:
resource.type="firebase_domain" httpRequest.status>=300 AND httpRequest.status<400
Examine the frequent sequences of the same
 
requestUrl
. In Firebase Hosting Usage (console), if “redirect” is listed, confirm its count. Identify any page that keeps issuing a 301/302 back and forth.
Mitigation:
 
Fix the redirect rules. In
 
firebase.json
, ensure your
 
redirects
 
are unambiguous. For example:
"redirects": [
  {"source": "/old-path", "destination": "/new-path", "type": 301},
  {"source": "/en", "destination": "https://soulamore.com", "type": 301}
]
Avoid circular patterns. In Nginx (if used), ensure your
 
return
 
or
 
rewrite
 
does not loop. After fixing, test with
 
curl -I
 
to ensure no chain loops.
Estimated Savings:
 
Eliminating loops removes the extra round-trip overhead. If each loop was adding ~200 KB per visit, stopping it directly cuts that egress.
6) Public Storage Buckets or Exposed Files
Issue:
 
If a Firebase Storage bucket or Google Cloud Storage is publicly readable (and linked from anywhere), anyone can download big files. These downloads generate egress (on Cloud Storage billing, not Hosting). Similar risk if an entire directory of static files (e.g. backups) is exposed.
How it inflates egress:
 
Unintended file sharing – e.g. PDFs, videos in Storage – can be crawled or shared. A single 100 MB file hit 100 times = 10 GB. Since Firebase Hosting and Storage are separate billing, large storage egress can also appear on your bill.
Detection:
 
Check Cloud Storage usage in the Google Cloud Console. Use Cloud Audit Logs (ensure they’re enabled) to see access events on buckets. In Logging, filter by
 
resource.type="gcs_bucket"
 
and large
 
size
. Also, search for URLs pointing to
 
storage.googleapis.com/your-bucket
 
in logs. Any spikes indicate public downloads.
Mitigation:
 
Lock down the bucket. In Firebase Storage rules, disallow unauthenticated reads unless required. Use authenticated access or signed URLs for files. Remove any
 
List
 
permission to prevent directory enumeration. If using Firebase Hosting to serve storage files, consider moving them behind Cloud Functions or Cloud Storage with signed URLs. For extra safety, set CORS to only allow expected origins.
Estimated Savings:
 
Locking a public 500 MB video could save 500 MB per unexpected hit. Overall, removing large public assets can remove multi-gigabyte leaks.
7) Bots Hitting Default
 
*.web.app
 
/
 
*.firebaseapp.com
 
Domains
Issue:
 
Firebase projects always have default domains (
soulamore.web.app
,
 
soulamore.firebaseapp.com
). Bots frequently probe these even when you use a custom domain (soulamore.com). Since Cloudflare (protecting
 
soulamore.com
) doesn’t cover these, malicious or automated bots can hit the default hostnames directly.
How it inflates egress:
 
Each bot request to the default domain still returns your site (or 404/redirect) and counts toward hosting bandwidth. We saw millions of bogus requests from a “meta-externalagent” crawler hitting the
 
.web.app
 
domain
[7]
.
Detection:
 
Use Cloud Logging and filter by
 
jsonPayload.hostname
. For example:
resource.type="firebase_domain" jsonPayload.hostname:"soulamore-f0a64"
Any hits where
 
hostname
 
ends with
 
.web.app
 
or
 
.firebaseapp.com
 
reveal this traffic. Look at
 
userAgent
 
fields for suspicious crawlers (e.g. known bot names). If traffic to default domains spikes, that’s the source.
Mitigation:
 
You cannot fully disable the domains
[8]
, but you can minimize their impact. At runtime (client-side), redirect these hostnames to your custom domain. For example, in your main HTML or Cloud Function:
if (window.location.hostname.endsWith(".web.app") ||
    window.location.hostname.endsWith(".firebaseapp.com")) {
  window.location.replace("https://www.soulamore.com");
}
However, better is at the network edge: use a
 
Cloudflare Worker
 
to intercept requests to the default domain and issue a 301 to
 
soulamore.com
. In the Firebase Console under
 
Hosting → Custom Domains
, remove any entries of
 
*.web.app
/
*.firebaseapp.com
 
(to require manual CNAME if possible). In Cloudflare, block known scraping bots by UA (e.g. rule
 
http.user_agent contains "meta-externalagent"
 
→ Block).
Estimated Savings:
 
In our case, disabling
 
.web.app
 
traffic blocked ~70 GB/day. Even if your bot traffic is smaller, cutting off default-domain bots can remove
 
most
 
of non-user traffic (often >80% of waste).
8) Preview/Deploy URLs Indexed
Issue:
 
Firebase’s preview/deploy URLs (like
 
soulamore--pr123.firebaseapp.com
 
or
 
soulamore-preview.hosting.link
) can be indexed by search engines or crawled if shared. These essentially duplicate your site content on separate hostnames, doubling traffic if crawled separately.
How it inflates egress:
 
Every visit to a preview URL (intended for testing) still serves your assets. If Google indexes it, it can lead to automated or user hits.
Detection:
 
In Cloud Logging, filter for any
 
hostname
 
that matches deploy or hosting link patterns. E.g.:
resource.type="firebase_domain" jsonPayload.requestUrl:("pr-" OR "hosting.link")
Also check Google Search Console and Analytics for any traffic on those domains.
Mitigation:
 
Immediately unpublish or delete any staging URLs not in use. Add the below to
 
robots.txt
 
in those deployments to block crawlers:
User-agent: *
Disallow: /
(Alternatively, use
 
robots_noindex
 
headers). In the Firebase Console under “Previews” or “Hosting > Releases”, remove any publish links. If using GitHub preview channels, disable publicly accessible previews.
Estimated Savings:
 
Removing indexing of previews can prevent extra crawling. If a preview URL got 100 hits/day at 5 MB/page, that’s 500 MB/day. While usually smaller, it completely eliminates unwanted visits.
9) CDN Origin Pull Misconfiguration
Issue:
 
If Cloudflare or another CDN is configured incorrectly (e.g. CNAME points to the wrong origin), every request might bounce through multiple layers or skip caching. An origin pull loop (CDN pulling from itself) can double-charge data.
How it inflates egress:
 
For example, if Cloudflare pulls from
 
soulamore.web.app
 
instead of
 
soulamore.com
, you effectively bypass your custom domain cache. Also, if DNS is mis-set (e.g. CNAME loop), requests may not cache.
Detection:
 
In Cloudflare DNS settings, ensure your site is proxied (orange-cloud icon) and points to
 
soulamore.com
 
(the actual origin). If you see multiple entries or odd A records, fix them. In logs, look for unusual request patterns or cycles. This issue is rare but important to check if none of the above applies.
Mitigation:
 
Correct your DNS/CNAME. For a Firebase site, typically you CNAME your custom domain to
 
ghs.googlehosted.com
 
(handled by Firebase) and enable proxy. If you used Cloudflare as the authoritative DNS, ensure the proxying is only on the custom zone, not on
 
.web.app
 
zones. No additional firebase.json change is needed.
Estimated Savings:
 
Mostly avoids subtle inefficiencies; proper CDN config ensures caching works and no accidental double-fetch.
10) Automated Vulnerability Scanners and DDoS
Issue:
 
Malicious scanners or DDoS bots can flood your site. Even if not causing actual outage, high-volume scanning (e.g. with tools like curl, Nikto, Arachni, or botnets) can drive up bandwidth.
How it inflates egress:
 
Thousands of hits from vulnerability scanners or DDoS bots, even for innocuous pages, accumulate gigabytes. These often use known UA strings (e.g.
 
Nikto
,
 
Arachni
,
 
sqlmap
,
 
masscan
).
Detection:
 
In Cloudflare’s Firewall Events or logs (or Firebase logs via Cloud Logging), look for repetitive hits with bot-like
 
userAgent
 
or high hit rates from a few IP ranges. Use log queries such as:
resource.type="firebase_domain" jsonPayload.userAgent:"Nikto"
or search for many 404s from the same IP. Also monitor requests/sec via Firebase Hosting metrics. An alert on >1000 requests/min from one IP or Geo can catch a DDoS.
Mitigation:
 
Enable Cloudflare’s DDoS/HTTP flood protection. Turn on
 
Bot Fight Mode
 
or
 
Super Bot Fight Mode
 
and
 
Rate Limiting
. Set a general rule to challenge or block suspicious UAs: e.g.
(http.user_agent contains "sqlmap" or "curl" or "Nikto" or "Arachni") 
→ block
Or simply use Cloudflare’s
 
User-Agent Blocking
. For high-rate traffic (like >200 reqs/min/IP), enable a Rate Limiting rule for “any path” to block the IP for some time. Also consider “Under Attack Mode” during peaks. Ensure App Check (Firebase) or reCAPTCHA for forms to prevent abuse.
Estimated Savings:
 
Blocking a DDoS or scanner early can save hundreds of GBs in a large attack. Even script kiddies can generate ~10 GB/day; stopping them preserves bandwidth and money.
